Research

My work connects three-dimensional flow organization with momentum transport, pressure and hydrodynamic loads. Ongoing work on boiling and bubble-driven convection extends these methods toward interfacial heat and mass transfer.

Freely moving bodies

Refractive-index matching makes transparent solids optically invisible, so the flow right next to a moving body can be imaged in three dimensions. I use time-resolved, four-camera tomographic PTV in a sodium-iodide solution to study spheres rising freely under buoyancy.

Because the matched spheres cannot be seen directly, a physics-informed detection method locates them from the tracer density, the velocity field and the vortex structures. The measurements resolve vortex shedding and wake evolution together with the sphere motion, and show a change of regime between the larger sphere and the smaller ones.

Key papers

  • Refractive index matching for rising spheres Int. J. Multiphase Flow 2026
  • Tomographic PTV around buoyancy-driven transparent solids Lisbon Symposium 2026

Boundary-aware reconstruction

Volumetric particle tracking gives scattered Lagrangian tracks that must be reconstructed on a grid before gradients can be computed. When a solid body sits inside the measurement volume, standard reconstructions treat the whole domain as fluid and are weakest right next to the body, where gradients are steepest.

I developed LE-DM, which classifies grid nodes as open fluid, boundary shell or solid at every instant and enforces incompressibility and the body’s surface velocity in a single solve. The same formulation handles stationary walls and translating, rotating, multiple or deforming bodies. I also built a five-camera system combining tomographic and PSF-engineered imaging, used to validate a single-camera 3D PIV method based on wavefront shaping and deep learning.

Key papers

  • LE-DM: dynamic masking for moving solids Meas. Sci. Technol. 2026
  • Monocular 3D PIV by wavefront shaping arXiv, under review

Turbulent separated flows

Separated flows behind steps and expansions control pressure loss, mixing and transport. Using refractive-index-matched stereo PIV, I compare abrupt (90°) and gradual (45°) axisymmetric pipe expansions at step-height Reynolds numbers of 25,000 and 35,000. The gradual expansion produces higher turbulence and stronger anisotropy, because its return flow follows the sloped wall and meets the free stream obliquely.

Time-resolved PIV, space–time correlations and Lagrangian coherent structures show that geometry changes how coherent structures are transported near separation, and that these differences fade toward reattachment. Related work examines how length and rear-face angle shape separation and turbulence production over surface-mounted protrusions.

Key papers

  • Effect of expansion geometry on turbulence Phys. Rev. Fluids 2026
  • Coherent structure transport in pipe expansions Phys. Rev. Fluids, accepted
  • Flow separation over trapezoidal protrusions arXiv, under review

Dynamic stall and unsteady loads

During my Ph.D. at Johns Hopkins, I studied a NACA 0015 airfoil pitching through dynamic stall over reduced frequencies of 0.047 to 1.571 and Reynolds numbers of 13,600 to 136,000. Refractive-index matching allowed stereo PIV on both sides of the transparent airfoil at once, and the pressure field was obtained by integrating the material acceleration.

Integrating the surface pressure gives the lift, pressure drag and moment, linking the dynamic-stall vortex directly to the loads. The vortex detaches at a fixed fraction of the oscillation period, and once time is shifted by that detachment time, the lift and drag curves collapse across conditions.

Key papers

  • Flow, pressure and loads during dynamic stall AIAA J. 2026

Shock-driven flows

Using the shock tube I designed and built at the Technion, together with large eddy simulations and geometrical shock dynamics, we studied how a normal shock wave (Mach 1.1 to 1.8) expanding through an abrupt area change, with area ratios up to 5, recovers into a uniform shock far downstream. A laterally moving shock that reverberates between the walls evens out the flow behind the shock front.

Key papers

  • Shock wave propagation past an area expansion J. Fluid Mech. 2024

Liquid–gas interfaces

Floating particles deform the liquid–gas interface, which drives capillary attraction and aggregation, the so-called Cheerios effect. With collaborators, we derived a closed-form solution for the interface shape around particles of complex, anisotropic shape.

To test it, I built a fluorescence-based laser-sheet scanning system with stereo imaging that maps the interface with 0.1 mm precision around 3D-printed circular, polygonal and undulated particles.

Key papers

  • Interface shape around anisotropic particles J. Fluid Mech. 2025
