About me

Hi, I’m Jibu. Thanks for stopping by.

I’m glad you found your way here. This is where I share my research, the experiments behind it, and the tools I develop along the way.

Who I am

I like to think of myself as both an engineer and a physicist. The engineer in me loves building things, whether it’s an experimental facility, a new instrument, or a measurement technique that makes a difficult experiment possible. The physicist in me wants to understand why things behave the way they do.

Most of my work lies somewhere between the two. I build experiments not simply to observe a phenomenon, but to answer a question about the physics behind it. Sometimes that means developing an entirely new setup or measurement method before I can even begin looking for answers.

I completed my Ph.D. in Mechanical Engineering at Johns Hopkins University and am currently a postdoctoral researcher in Mechanical Engineering at the Technion.

What I study

I’m fascinated by unsteady flows, particularly those in which seemingly simple conditions give rise to surprisingly complex behavior. Vortices forming and shedding from moving bodies, shear layers evolving into turbulence, shock waves bending and reflecting through confined passages, or liquid surfaces deforming around floating objects.

These phenomena may look very different, but they share a common thread. I’m interested in how flows organize themselves, how that organization evolves, and how it influences the forces and transport processes around them.

What drives me

Much of my research is motivated by a simple idea. If we can measure a flow well enough, we can begin to understand what makes it behave the way it does.

I try to capture flows as completely as possible, in three dimensions, over time, and as close to solid boundaries and interfaces as the experiments allow. That often means developing new experimental techniques, building specialized facilities, or finding better ways to extract information from measurements.

But a good measurement should do more than produce a beautiful picture. It should reveal a physical mechanism, test a model, and provide something that others can build upon.

This is also why I enjoy developing and sharing open-source research tools. I believe the methods we create along the way can be just as useful to the research community as the scientific results themselves.

Outside the lab

When I’m not working, you’ll probably find me gaming, traveling, or taking photographs.

I enjoy exploring places where nature and local culture come together. I’d much rather wander through a quiet village, discover an unfamiliar trail, or stumble upon a hidden gem than follow a crowded tourist itinerary. I enjoy capturing the little things that catch my eye along the way, usually with nothing more than my phone.

Photography is my way of slowing down and noticing details I might otherwise overlook. And now that I think about it, that isn’t so different from what I do in the lab.

Thanks for visiting

Feel free to explore my research, browse the publications, or take a look at the tools I’ve developed. If something catches your interest, raises a question, or sparks an idea for collaboration, I’d be happy to hear from you.
